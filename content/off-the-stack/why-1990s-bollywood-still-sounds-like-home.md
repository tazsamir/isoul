Some songs remind me of a particular moment. Others bring back a whole period of life.

1990s Bollywood does the second one.

I cannot give you a tidy story about the first time I heard every song. Most of them have been around for too long. I know what happens when one comes on, though. A familiar voice arrives, I recognise the arrangement, and the distance between then and now gets a little shorter.

## The voices were the map

I cannot separate the music of that decade from its playback singers.

Udit Narayan could make a song sound bright and hopeful without losing its softness. Kumar Sanu had a heavier romantic sound, unmistakable within a few words. Alka Yagnik could meet either of them as an equal rather than disappearing into the arrangement. Lata Mangeshkar connected those films to a much longer musical history.

The actors, stories and composers changed, but those voices kept returning. They became a way of finding my bearings in the decade.

I might forget parts of a film or go years without watching it. A voice can bring its mood back before I have even placed the song.

## The soundtrack could outgrow the film

Bollywood songs were never background decoration. They carried the parts of a story that ordinary dialogue could not: anticipation, celebration, heartbreak and those enormous declarations of love that would sound ridiculous anywhere else.

*Jo Jeeta Wohi Sikandar* is hard for me to separate from “Pehla Nasha.” The song escaped the film long ago. It now carries its own version of romantic nostalgia, even for people who have not revisited the rest of the soundtrack in years.

*Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge* works differently. Its soundtrack feels like a complete expression of 1990s Bollywood: melodic, expansive and completely unembarrassed by emotion. I can hear one song and immediately want to play the others.

I like that lack of embarrassment. These songs do not hide affection behind irony, and they rarely hurry towards the chorus as if the listener might otherwise leave. They take their time.

## I do not want old recordings made new

Returning to these soundtracks, I notice how much room they give the melody. Introductions can run for a while. Instrumental passages have a purpose. A duet feels like two people responding to each other rather than two vocal tracks fitted into the same template.

The recordings also sound like their time. That is part of why I love them.

A clean remaster can be welcome, especially when an older release sounds thin or damaged. But I do not want every favourite polished until it pretends to have been recorded yesterday. The production, instruments and vocal style belong to the memory too. Good restoration should let me hear the original more clearly, not replace it with a modern imitation.

## Looking after the music

This is where the subject joins the rest of this site.

Keeping a personal music collection is partly practical. Albums can disappear from streaming services, rights can move between labels, and the version that remains online is not always the version you knew. A local copy gives the collection some stability.

There is a more personal reason as well. A streaming catalogue can offer almost everything, but it cannot tell me which part matters.

My own library can. The soundtracks are there because I chose to keep them. I correct the metadata, separate releases that have been bundled together and look for artwork that belongs to the right edition. It is fussy work, but I would rather do it once than have a favourite album scattered across a service under three slightly different artist names.

That curation also leaves something understandable behind. A folder full of mystery files is technically a copy. A named, organised album is part of a collection.

## Still listening

I listen to newer music too. This is not an argument that songs stopped being good after the 1990s, or that nostalgia is a substitute for taste.

New music surprises me. The older songs locate me.

That is why I keep returning to these singers and soundtracks. They remind me that a music library is not a chart of the records I think are best. It is an uneven history of what stayed with me.

Sometimes all it takes is Udit Narayan beginning a line or Alka Yagnik answering one. A film I have not watched for years feels close again, and the music still sounds like home.
