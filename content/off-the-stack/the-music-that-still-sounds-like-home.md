When I think about music that sounds like home, Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan is at the centre of it.

There was plenty of Bollywood music too. Udit Narayan, Kumar Sanu, Alka Yagnik and Lata Mangeshkar were familiar voices, and film soundtracks became part of the atmosphere rather than something that needed to be deliberately chosen. Bally Sagoo belonged there as well, taking songs people already knew and placing them inside a British Asian sound that felt connected to more than one world.

It was not one tidy genre or one kind of listening. Qawwali, film songs, remixes and pop all sat near each other. Looking back, that mixture makes more sense to me than any carefully organised category ever could.

## Nusrat at the centre

I usually shorten his name to NFAK. The initials are convenient, but nothing about the music itself feels abbreviated.

It is difficult to leave Nusrat playing in the background. Even when a performance begins gently, there is a sense that it is heading somewhere. The harmonium establishes the melody, the clapping finds its pulse and the responding voices build a frame around him. Then his voice pushes through it.

The power is obvious, but power alone does not explain why I return to it. He could stretch a line, soften it, repeat it and come back with a completely different weight. A phrase that seemed settled a minute earlier could suddenly sound urgent.

His longer performances were also allowed to unfold at their own pace. Repetition was part of the movement. A line returned until its meaning changed, or perhaps until the listener did. “Yeh Jo Halka Halka Suroor Hai” and “Tumhe Dillagi” did not need to hurry towards the part somebody might extract for a clip. The build was the point.

## Bally Sagoo sounded like here

Bally Sagoo brought a different energy.

His music made older South Asian songs feel at home beside the bass, drums and production of British clubs and radio. It did not ask listeners to choose between inherited music and the place where they were growing up. Both could occupy the same track.

That mattered. A remix could keep the melody or vocal that made a song familiar while changing the setting around it. The result was not simply an old song made louder. At its best, it reflected how music was already being heard: across generations, formats and cultures that did not stay in separate boxes.

Albums such as *Bollywood Flashback* made that approach explicit. The title looked backwards, but the sound belonged to its own moment. Bally Sagoo was not preserving the songs behind glass. He was putting them back into circulation for another audience.

There is a particular pleasure in hearing a familiar vocal arrive over production that immediately dates itself to the 1990s. I do not mean that as an insult. The drum sounds, keyboards and bass are part of the character. Cleaning away every sign of the period would clean away some of the memory too.

## Bollywood was everywhere around it

The film music still matters.

*Jo Jeeta Wohi Sikandar* is hard for me to separate from “Pehla Nasha.” The song has long since escaped the boundaries of the film and become its own piece of romantic nostalgia.

*Dilwale Dulhania Le Jayenge* works differently. Its soundtrack feels like a complete expression of 1990s Bollywood: melodic, expansive and completely unembarrassed by emotion. I can hear one song and immediately want to play the others.

Those playback voices linked films that otherwise had different actors, stories and composers. Udit Narayan could make a song sound bright and hopeful without losing its softness. Kumar Sanu had a heavier romantic sound that was unmistakable within a few words. Alka Yagnik could meet either of them as an equal rather than disappearing into the arrangement.

The songs took their time. Introductions could run for a while, instrumental passages had room to breathe and a duet felt like two people responding to one another. They were built for films, but the best of them never needed the pictures to survive.

## Looking after the music

Keeping this music in a personal library can be untidy.

Nusrat's name and song titles are transliterated in different ways. The same performance can appear on several releases with different titles, lengths or artwork. A shortened recording may sit beside a much longer one without a clear explanation. Remixes add another layer because the singer, original composer and producer can all be credited inconsistently.

Streaming services do not always make those relationships easier to understand.

That makes curation useful. I want to know which recording I am playing, whether it is complete and where it belongs. Correcting an album title or separating two releases is mundane work, but it stops the collection from becoming a pile of near-duplicates.

This is one reason digital ownership matters to me. Saving the audio is only the first step. Keeping the context makes it possible to return to the music without depending on whichever version a service happens to present that day.

## Still sounding like home

I listen to newer music too. This is not an argument that everything became worse after the 1990s, or that nostalgia can turn every old recording into a masterpiece.

These songs simply do a different job. New music can surprise me. Older music can place me.

Nusrat remains at the centre, with Bally Sagoo and all that Bollywood music around him. Together they form something broader than a favourites list. They preserve the overlap between family, culture and memory without asking those things to remain separate.

The equipment has changed. The formats have changed. The music still sounds like home.
