## The life around the main story

*Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix* follows Harry through his fifth year at Hogwarts after Voldemort's return. Much of the wizarding world does not want to believe him. The Ministry begins interfering heavily with Hogwarts, Harry becomes increasingly isolated and angry, and the school year turns into a mixture of exams, politics, friendships, rumours, punishment and resistance. At the same time, strange dreams and visions seem to connect him more closely to Voldemort.

This is probably the point where the books really start pulling away from the films for me. The film reaches the same broad destination, but the book has so much more connective tissue. It spends time on ordinary school life, Quidditch, prefects, revision, careers, teenage jealousy, awkward relationships, family visits and school politics. All of this continues while something much darker is happening in the background.

The full-cast audiobook helps enormously. The arguments feel sharper, the comedy lands better and the Ministry scenes have real energy. Characters who feel reduced in the films, especially Ginny, Neville, Luna, Ron, Hermione and even Sirius, get much more room to breathe. The book is long, but by the end I understood why. A lot of what makes it work is not the main plot itself, but everything happening around it.

> **Major spoilers for *Harry Potter and the Order of the Phoenix* follow.**

## Harry's anger and a Ministry in denial

Harry's anger makes much more sense here than it does in the film. He is not simply moody for the sake of it. He is fifteen, grieving, being publicly called a liar, kept in the dark by people he trusts and expected to behave rationally while the adults around him repeatedly fail to explain what is happening. Even his reaction to Ron becoming a prefect has more nuance than I remembered. Harry is jealous and knows it, but he also knows Ron has done nothing wrong and tries to be happy for him. It is a surprisingly mature piece of internal dialogue.

The Ministry hearing shows how far the authorities will go to discredit him. Dragging Harry in front of the full Wizengamot over underage magic is absurdly disproportionate, which makes Dumbledore walking in and dismantling the case immensely satisfying. Amelia Bones being genuinely interested in Harry producing a corporeal Patronus is a lovely touch. For a moment, it cuts through the political nonsense and recognises that Harry has done something very difficult.

Fudge becomes more infuriating as the book goes on. He has enough evidence to suspect that Harry and Dumbledore may be telling the truth, but accepting it would mean admitting that his whole understanding of the situation is wrong. He attacks the people warning him instead. Add Lucius Malfoy's money, access and political connections, and it is hard not to conclude that the Ministry is rotten.

Umbridge is the clearest expression of that rot. For most of the book she is somehow more infuriating than Voldemort because she represents a more familiar kind of evil: bureaucracy, smiling cruelty, rules used as weapons and power justified by procedure. Her treatment of Harry is abuse dressed up as discipline. What she does to Hagrid and Trelawney is cruel, and the most disturbing part is that she believes the system gives her the right to do it.

That is why McGonagall opposing her is so satisfying. The career advice scene is one of my favourites: Harry says he wants to become an Auror, Umbridge tries to undermine him, and McGonagall effectively decides that she will personally make sure he gets there. "Cleared of all charges" is a wonderful line. I also loved how much McGonagall cares about Quidditch. She is strict and controlled most of the time, but the sport brings out another side of her.

## Hogwarts under pressure

The book makes Hogwarts feel more like a real British secondary school than ever. The O.W.L.s are basically GCSEs, so the year gradually fills with revision, career advice, exam panic and teachers constantly talking about grades. Everyone sits the exams and goes home before receiving the results. Very familiar. The grading system is wonderfully Hogwarts too: Outstanding, Exceeds Expectations, Acceptable, Poor, Dreadful and Troll.

Ron and Hermione being prefects adds to that sense of a lived-in school. Ron tries to control younger students while knowing there is no point attempting to discipline Fred and George. Harry not being made a prefect suits him. He is an unwilling troublemaker: trouble finds him, and then he reacts.

Ron's Quidditch story is much better in the book as well. "Weasley is our King" destroys his confidence at first, until he starts second-guessing everything and becomes his own worst enemy. When he finally turns it around, keeps the Quaffle out and has the song reclaimed in his favour, the payoff feels earned.

The DA works for the same reason. It feels like an actual student movement rather than a montage. Different year groups attend, people are bad at spells before they improve, and Harry discovers that he is genuinely good at teaching. Ron and Hermione laugh when he underrates himself, then remind him of everything he has already done. Hermione, meanwhile, might quietly be the most dangerous student at Hogwarts. She creates the DA, convinces Harry to teach, uses a Protean Charm on the fake Galleons, jinxes the parchment and finds a way to publish Harry's account when the mainstream wizarding press will not touch it. She thinks strategically while Harry usually acts emotionally, and somehow still finds time to revise. Fabulous Hermione.

Dobby leading Harry to the Room of Requirement is better world-building than the film's version. Hermione leaves knitted clothing around Gryffindor Tower, the house-elves refuse to clean there, and Dobby takes over. Harry later asks him for help, and Dobby tells him about the room. That chain of events makes the world feel connected rather than arranged as a series of plot points. The room itself is one of my favourite magical ideas in the series. Harry needs somewhere to teach and the castle gives him one; Hermione walks in, sees shelves of books and is immediately delighted. Of course she is.

Fred and George are fantastic here too. Their inventions are no longer just jokes. They are effectively running product development, testing and selling Skiving Snackboxes, fireworks and portable swamps. They are clearly intelligent; they simply do not care about applying that intelligence in the way school expects. Their departure is one of the most satisfying scenes in the book, and Peeves listening to them afterwards is about the highest endorsement they could receive.

## Ginny, Luna and Neville step forward

Ginny finally feels like Ginny in this book. She stops being Ron's younger sister who happens to like Harry and becomes her own character: confident, blunt and funny, with her own friends and ideas. She stands up for Neville, has practised flying behind her brothers' backs, becomes Seeker and catches the Snitch. Most importantly, she talks to Harry normally instead of becoming embarrassed whenever he enters the room. The later Harry and Ginny relationship makes much more sense in the books because this version of Ginny actually exists.

Luna is brilliant from the moment she appears. The description of her having an "aura of distinct dottiness" is wonderful, and "You're just as sane as I am" is funny precisely because everyone already thinks she is mad. She reads and believes strange things, laughs loudly at Ron and can be blunt about Hagrid, yet she is often the person who sees through everyone else's nonsense.

She also gives Harry one of the gentlest moments in the book. Harry is desperate for Sirius to come back, while Luna believes that the people beyond the Veil have not simply been erased. They may be gone from here without being gone altogether. She does not give him proof, only the possibility that death is separation rather than annihilation. After everything that has happened, that is exactly the kind of comfort he needs.

Neville may have my favourite development in the entire book. The St Mungo's scene changes how I see him. Meeting his parents and hearing his grandmother speak about them explains the weight he carries. He is constantly measured against his father, but makes it clear that he is not ashamed of either parent.

Then he keeps practising in the DA. He does not suddenly become perfect; he improves because he works at it. At the Ministry he still misses spells and makes mistakes, but he keeps going. He manages a double Expelliarmus, gets injured and ends up crawling after Harry rather than leaving him. Neville is not brave because he suddenly becomes the strongest wizard in the room. He is brave because he is terrified, injured and outmatched, yet refuses to leave his friends behind.

## Growing up means seeing people clearly

Snape continues to be fascinating, and his dialogue in the audiobook is fantastic. Everything sounds controlled, sharp and almost poetic: "Did you read line three, Potter?"; "Manners, Potter"; "Sit down, Potter." The Occlumency lessons are particularly interesting because Dumbledore is right that Harry needs them. Voldemort is trying to exploit their connection, and Snape is skilled enough to teach Harry. Putting those two together, however, is almost the worst possible emotional arrangement. Harry is told to clear his mind while spending every lesson with someone who knows exactly how to make him furious. Very Vulcan.

Then Harry sees Snape's worst memory. James and Sirius are awful in that scene. Snape is not doing anything to them; they simply decide to humiliate him. Lupin knows it is wrong and does not stop them. Harry having to face the fact that his father could be a bully is a great moment. It does not excuse what Snape later becomes. Both things can be true: Snape can have been a victim and can grow into an adult who is cruel to children.

Harry and Cho's relationship also benefits from the book having enough time to let them be awkward teenagers. They like each other, but neither of them knows what they are doing. Harry is dealing with Voldemort, Cedric, Umbridge, Dumbledore avoiding him, nightmares, schoolwork and half the country calling him a liar. Somehow he is expected to understand relationships as well. Ron and Hermione questioning him about the kiss is hilarious, as is Hermione telling Ron that he has the emotional range of a teaspoon. Cho's defence of Marietta and pointed comments about Hermione feel like jealousy tangled up with loyalty. Harry could save himself pain by admitting that he is completely out of his emotional depth, but then he would not be Harry.

The Quibbler plot gives Hermione another chance to show how well she understands people and systems. She does not suddenly decide that the magazine's conspiracy stories are respectable. She realises that if the mainstream press refuses Harry's story, the strange publication will give him access to an audience. It is such a Hermione solution.

## A wizarding world beyond the plot

St Mungo's is one of my favourite pieces of world-building in the series because it makes magical Britain feel like a society rather than Hogwarts plus the Ministry. The visit moves naturally between comedy, sadness and ordinary family life: Arthur pretending the snake attack is not serious, Mrs Weasley thanking Harry, a portrait chasing Ron while claiming his freckles are a disease, and Lockhart still somehow being Lockhart after losing his memory. The healer Smethwyck's name immediately made me think of Smethwick. Then Neville and his parents change the mood completely without making the earlier comedy feel misplaced.

The book made me think more about Squibs too. Imagine being born into a magical family, knowing magic is real and watching everyone around you perform it while being unable to use it yourself. That must be brutal. Mrs Figg is also much funnier than I remembered, especially when Harry keeps his wand out while she complains about Mundungus and swings a bag of cat food around.

The Thestrals are another great idea. Harry can see them now because Cedric's death has finally registered and been processed; Luna can see them too. Hagrid's lesson is genuinely interesting until Umbridge arrives to ruin it, and later the creatures become transport. A group of teenagers climbing onto invisible skeletal horses and flying to London because they believe someone needs rescuing is exactly the sort of thing teenagers in this world would do. Kids think they are invincible.

I continue to love the ordinary magic as well. Hot-Air Charms clear snow, Accio summons Butterbeer and Bubble-Head Charms solve practical problems. The familiar defensive spells then become useful in real danger. Petrificus Totalus is a nice callback to the first book: Hermione once used this long, memorable spell on Neville as a first-year, while Harry now uses it in combat as part of his normal toolkit. The series does not always need a completely new spell. School-level magic can become valuable when the situation changes. It is still strange that Hogwarts does not teach basic magical first aid. Surely learning how not to bleed to death should rank somewhere above Divination.

Even the examiners add depth. Griselda Marchbanks remembers examining Dumbledore for his N.E.W.T.s and seeing him do things with a wand she had never seen before. She is not repeating a legend; she remembers him as a student. Harry receives his own smaller moment of recognition when an examiner asks him to demonstrate a Patronus. After a year of being called a liar, unstable or attention-seeking, a competent adult assesses him and recognises that he is unusually good at defensive magic.

## The trap at the Department of Mysteries

The Department of Mysteries battle is fantastic in the audiobook. The performance becomes frantic, spells fly everywhere, doors open into bizarre rooms, and all that DA practice is tested in a real fight. The difference between practising in the Room of Requirement and facing Death Eaters becomes obvious immediately. The Time Room is wonderfully strange, one Death Eater ends up with a baby's head, and the brains in tanks are horrifying while somehow making me think of *Futurama*.

Lucius behaves like a field commander: controlled and focused on retrieving the prophecy. Bellatrix is much more volatile. Harry, meanwhile, has the nerve to taunt her about Voldemort being a half-blood while surrounded by Death Eaters. That boy sometimes has no survival instinct at all.

The prophecy is more interesting with the full explanation. It could originally have referred to Harry or Neville: both were born at the end of July to parents who had defied Voldemort three times. Voldemort chose Harry. By trying to kill him, he marked Harry as his equal and helped create the situation he wanted to prevent. It is a great self-fulfilling prophecy.

Sirius's death hurts because his behaviour just before it is so completely Sirius. He is brave but reckless, showing off while fighting Bellatrix and taunting her with "You can do better than that." Then he is gone. Harry refuses to accept it and keeps looking for him because he cannot process how somebody can be standing there one second and disappear the next.

Harry really did try to verify the vision before going to the Ministry. Hermione is right about his tendency to play the hero, but he does not rush away without checking. He breaks into Umbridge's office, uses the fireplace and tries to contact Sirius. Kreacher lies. That makes the outcome much more tragic. Voldemort exploits Harry's instinct to save people, but the plan only succeeds because Harry receives false information at exactly the wrong moment.

## Dumbledore, Voldemort and what the film loses

Dumbledore's explanation after Sirius's death is one of the best conversations in the book. He was right that Voldemort could use the mental connection, and right that Harry needed Occlumency. He also admits that keeping Harry at a distance was his own mistake. He refused to look at him, withheld information and tried to protect him by keeping him ignorant. In doing so, he helped create the circumstances that led to Sirius's death. The "old man's mistake" lands because Dumbledore is not stupid. His mistake comes from love, fear and too much faith in his own judgement. Poor Harry. Poor Dumbledore.

The confrontation between Dumbledore and Voldemort contains one of the most interesting exchanges in the series so far. Voldemort believes there is nothing worse than death. That belief explains his fear of dying, obsession with immortality and willingness to damage his own soul. Dumbledore's worldview is the opposite. Losing your humanity, destroying your soul, living without love and becoming incapable of remorse are all worse. Voldemort will pay any price to avoid death; Dumbledore cares more about the person you become before it arrives.

The duel makes Dumbledore feel completely different from everyone else. Most people fire spells at one another; he controls the room, animates statues, moves objects and protects Harry. A statue physically takes a curse for Harry, answering something I had wondered earlier. Avada Kedavra cannot be stopped with an ordinary Protego, so Dumbledore puts a physical magical object in its path. Sometimes the best defence is not a stronger shield but making sure the curse hits something else.

I still think wizarding society should spend more time researching a reliable defence against the Killing Curse. Magic evolves, people invent spells and the Department of Mysteries exists to investigate what is not understood. Somebody should treat Avada Kedavra as a magical problem that needs engineering around, even if the answer is not a simple shield. It reminds me of *Frieren*: something that once seems overwhelmingly powerful can become manageable after enough time and study.

This is where my biggest feeling about the film returns. The film tells the main story, but the book tells the life around it. It has Ron and Hermione as prefects, Ron's Quidditch crisis, Ginny becoming a person in her own right, Dobby finding the Room of Requirement, St Mungo's, Neville's parents, the full Snape memory, a proper Harry and Cho relationship, the O.W.L. year, Firenze teaching Divination, the Quibbler plot, Harry trying to contact Sirius and Dumbledore fully admitting his mistakes. The film arrives at roughly the same destination. The book makes the journey matter.

*Order of the Phoenix* is about Voldemort's return, but also institutions refusing reality, teenagers dealing with grief, adults making mistakes while trying to protect children, school pressure, propaganda, power and resistance. Somehow it is still incredibly funny. Fred and George, Peeves, Ron, Luna, McGonagall, Dobby and the ridiculous portraits keep Hogwarts alive while everything around Harry gets darker. The ending is painful, especially Sirius's death, but Luna's thoughts about the Veil give it a quiet softness. People may be gone from here. That does not necessarily mean they are gone entirely.
