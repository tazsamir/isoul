> **Major spoilers for *Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire* follow.**

I have finished the full-cast audiobook of *Harry Potter and the Goblet of Fire*, and this might be the book where I finally understood how much the films had to leave behind.

I knew the main story, of course. The Triwizard Tournament, the graveyard, Voldemort's return: none of that was new to me. What surprised me was how much life exists between those events. There are more conversations, more side stories and much more time inside Harry's head. Hogwarts feels busy in a way it rarely does on screen.

The full cast makes a real difference. Even somebody such as Professor Trelawney, who might only appear briefly, arrives with a voice and a personality of her own. It stops the school feeling like a backdrop occupied by Harry, Ron, Hermione and whichever teacher the plot needs that day.

## Harry is very obviously fourteen

My favourite discovery from listening to the books has been Harry himself. He is witty, sarcastic, stubborn and occasionally completely clueless. He can be brave without seeming like a capital-H Hero all the time.

The first task sums him up rather well. Faced with a dragon, Harry spends nearly an entire day and night practising *Accio* because he knows his life may depend on it. When the Yule Ball comes round, the idea of learning to dance apparently never crosses his mind.

He and Ron really should have practised.

Harry could have asked Parvati what she wanted from the evening. He could have asked Hermione for advice. He could even have asked Professor McGonagall, which might have been one of the rare occasions when going to McGonagall would have saved him rather than terrified him. Instead, he and Ron invite Parvati and Padma, barely dance with them and spend most of the night being miserable.

Parvati deserved better. It is rude, awkward and painfully believable behaviour from two teenage boys.

I also felt a little sorry for Hermione when Harry was thinking about whom to ask. She spends so much of the year helping him, yet he does not really consider her. At the same time, I cannot be too harsh on him. He has been entered into a deadly tournament, half the school thinks he cheated his way into it, Ron has only just started speaking to him again and he cannot think beyond Cho Chang. He is fourteen. Of course he handles it badly.

## Hogwarts has people in it

The number of students drifting in and out of the story changes the feeling of the school. Parvati, Padma, Lavender, Dean, Seamus, Neville, Lee Jordan, Angelina, Katie, Alicia and the Creeveys all gossip, argue, date, support different champions and get on with lives that do not revolve entirely around Harry.

Even Moaning Myrtle carries on existing beyond *Chamber of Secrets*. Her appearance in the Black Lake is hilarious. She manages to be helpful and intrusive at exactly the same time, then casually adds, "I don't like them," about the merpeople. It is a tiny moment, but the book is full of them.

Dobby benefits even more from the extra room. The films largely put him away after *Chamber of Secrets*, whereas the books allow him to become Harry's strange, loyal and genuinely useful friend. His name for Ron, "Wheezy", still makes me laugh.

The second task shows why his continued presence matters. Harry and his friends search through piles of books for a way to breathe underwater, then Dobby turns up like the world's most chaotic research assistant carrying Gillyweed. Harry, having stayed up for his last-minute revision session, promptly oversleeps and has to be woken by him. It is a wonderfully Harry and Dobby way to begin a dangerous task.

We also visit the kitchens, which connects Dobby and Winky to Hermione's campaign for house-elf rights. S.P.E.W. is often funny, not least because Hermione has the subtlety of a Bludger when she believes she is right, but I agree with her. Slavery does not become acceptable because it is old or normalised.

One line stayed with me: Hermione describing *Hogwarts: A History* as revisionist. This is the girl who once treated books as unquestionable authorities. Now she is asking who wrote them, what they left out and why. That feels like real growth.

## Moral fibre, a stone and a lot of panic

The second task might be my favourite part of the Tournament because it shows exactly who Harry is when nobody gives him time to think.

He reaches the hostages, cannot persuade the merpeople to lend him a spear, and starts hacking at the ropes with a stone. It is not elegant magic. It is Harry using the first thing that might work.

Then he refuses to leave Gabrielle behind. He has misunderstood the song and genuinely thinks the hostages may die, so the Tournament no longer matters. He is going to get everybody out if he can.

The best part comes afterwards. Once he realises they were never in danger and sees Ron returning with Gabrielle, he begins wondering why on earth he bothered. That is so completely Harry. He does not make a grand decision to demonstrate his goodness; he panics, tries to save everyone, then feels slightly foolish when the adults call it moral fibre.

Viktor Krum is much more likeable here too. He is quiet, awkward and surprisingly sincere. When he asks Harry about Hermione, the famous international Quidditch player suddenly sounds like any other insecure teenager trying to work out whether somebody likes him. The book allows him to be a person rather than just the famous athlete Hermione brings to the Ball.

## The mystery has room to breathe

Knowing the truth about Moody made listening to the book especially enjoyable. His strange behaviour works the first time as eccentricity and paranoia. Once you know who he really is, every helpful suggestion has another meaning. He keeps nudging Harry in precisely the direction needed to reach the Cup.

The book lets us trace the machinery backwards. We find out how Barty Crouch Jr helped Harry through the Tournament, how he watched everyone and why he always seemed to know what was happening. His conversation with Harry after the graveyard is excellent, but the explanations in Dumbledore's office and the hospital wing add even more. The film gives us the reveal; the book lets us see how it was done.

Rita Skeeter's secret works for the same reason. Her beetle form explains how she keeps learning things she should not know. She is awful, but very entertaining, and her articles give Hermione a taste of the scrutiny Harry has lived with for years. Strangers decide who you are from something they read, then stare and whisper as if they know you.

Barty Crouch Sr feels much stranger in the book as well. He disappears for long stretches, so his sudden appearance at Hogwarts, confused and broken, feels deeply wrong. Sirius's account of the Crouch family, the old trials and the first war fills in history that the film can only rush past.

It also made me think about how much Sirius knows. He missed years of ordinary life in Azkaban, but his young adulthood was consumed by the first war, the Order, the Potters, the Black family, Death Eaters and Ministry politics. This is the part of the world he understands intimately.

The same is true of Wormtail, in a nastier way. It is easy to dismiss him as pathetic because he attaches himself to stronger people, but he is not magically weak. He became an Animagus as a teenager, caused the explosion that helped him frame Sirius and kills Cedric with the Killing Curse. His problem is not a lack of ability. It is what he chooses to do with it.

## The graveyard sounds different

The graveyard scene carries far more weight with all that history behind it. This is Little Hangleton, where Tom Riddle's Muggle father and grandparents are buried. Voldemort rebuilds himself in a place tied to the part of his own family he despises. His obsession with blood purity is ugly enough already; knowing how much of that hatred is aimed at his own background makes it worse.

The connection between Harry's and Voldemort's wands is also properly explained. The people emerging from Voldemort's wand no longer feel like a mysterious visual effect. There are rules behind what is happening, and that gives Harry's escape more emotional weight.

The moment that really got me, though, was the phoenix song.

Cedric is dead. Voldemort is back. Harry is surrounded by Death Eaters and completely outmatched. Then he hears the song. He is still terrified, but it gives him enough strength to stand there and resist.

This is where the audiobook can do something the page and film cannot do in quite the same way. Sound is already carrying so much of the scene, so hearing that song cut through everything gave me goosebumps.

## Dumbledore takes charge

This book has also changed how I see Dumbledore. He finally feels like a leader rather than a distant, kindly headmaster who appears when Harry needs an answer.

After Harry returns from the graveyard, Dumbledore controls the room. He gathers information, gives orders and decides what must happen next. He is still calm and patient with Harry, but there is a weight to him when things become serious. You understand why people listen.

The scenes in Moody's office, Dumbledore's office and the hospital wing give the adults room to react. They protect students, argue over what has happened and begin preparing for what comes next. The world has started moving, and the teachers are part of it.

That is also why I loved the phrase "wands at the ready" so much. It sounds like something Aurors would say before entering a dangerous house. Those four words immediately had me imagining another corner of this world: magical investigations, old Death Eater networks, Ministry politics, raids and magical forensics. I would happily listen to an Auror series that began with those words.

## Everything is getting bigger

The book keeps bringing earlier details back. Grindylows return after being introduced in class. Sirius tells us about Barty Crouch Jr. Rita's impossible access has an explanation. Dobby and Winky matter. Fred and George's joke shop is already taking shape through product tests and plans that began during the summer. What first looks like two boys causing chaos is also a business being built in the background.

By the time Harry leaves Hogwarts, he has changed too. The Tournament forced him to practise spells, learn hexes and think under pressure. He is still only fourteen, but he is not quite the boy who began the school year.

This is where *Harry Potter* begins to feel much larger to me. Hogwarts still has lessons, jokes, Quidditch and painfully awkward teenage behaviour, but the story now has international wizarding culture, journalism, prejudice, Ministry failure, an old war that never really went away and a new one beginning.

The film keeps the main events. The book gives those events a world to happen in.

I loved *Prisoner of Azkaban*, but *Goblet of Fire* opens everything up. Harry feels more human, Hermione is bolder, Sirius has a history, Dobby becomes a proper recurring friend and Dumbledore finally sounds like the person everyone says he is.

And now, whenever somebody says "wands at the ready", I want that Auror spin-off all over again.
