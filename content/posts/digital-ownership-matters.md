---
title: "Why digital ownership matters"
date: 2026-09-11
description: "I learned the difference between having access to something and keeping control of it."
---

A disc on a shelf is not perfect, but it is understandable. I can see it, lend it, play it without signing in and decide when to replace it. A digital item tied to an account, region, server or changing licence is different: I may have paid for access without receiving lasting control.

For a long time, that distinction felt mostly theoretical to me. Then I had to recover years of family photographs.

## Access is not ownership

During a house move, I reset my NAS believing that another copy of the data gave me enough protection. It did not. Some of what I thought of as a replica still depended on encryption keys and recovery information that were not stored as carefully as the files themselves.

The photographs were not abstract data. They included family memories going back years. Recovering them meant piecing together several imperfect sources: an older storage system, cloud backups, database information and files that had survived elsewhere. No single source contained a neat, complete answer.

I was fortunate. Enough of those sources overlapped to recover the photographs. The experience changed what digital ownership meant to me.

Ownership is not merely having a file today. It means being able to find it, open it, move it and recover it when the original device, service or plan fails. An encrypted copy without its key is not under my control. A synchronised deletion is not a backup. A library that exists only inside one account is available on someone else's terms.

## Convenience still has value

The answer is not to hoard everything, reject every cloud service or insist that every application must run at home. Hosted services can be reliable, convenient and far easier to maintain. I still use them where the trade-off makes sense.

What I no longer want is a single point of failure for anything irreplaceable. Convenience should be one layer, not the only layer.

For me, that means:

- keeping local copies of personal files and purchases where practical;
- preferring common formats and services with a usable export;
- storing encryption keys and recovery information separately from the systems they unlock;
- maintaining more than one kind of backup;
- checking that a sample restore actually opens;
- knowing which devices and media still work without an account or internet connection; and
- keeping a simple inventory of what matters and where its copies live.

## Control is something you test

A backup dashboard can be green while the restore procedure remains unknown. A second machine can contain a copy while still sharing the same mistake as the first. Paying for storage does not prove that I can recover from it.

The useful test is deliberately ordinary: choose a photograph or document, restore it somewhere separate, open it and confirm that it is the file expected. Check that the necessary password or key can be found without relying on the machine being recovered. Repeat that often enough that recovery is a familiar task rather than an emergency experiment.

Digital ownership is not total independence. I do not need to manufacture the hardware, write the software or disconnect from every provider. I need enough control that one company, one account, one device or one mistake cannot erase the only copy of something important.

That is the lesson I kept from the recovery: access is temporary unless I have an exit, and a copy only becomes meaningful when I know I can restore it.
